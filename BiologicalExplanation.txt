TMO explained for wet‑lab biologists
====================================

TMO (Temporal Multi‑Omics) is a deep‑learning method that learns how the
timing of chromatin accessibility (ATAC) and gene expression (RNA) are
related in single cells. It answers: “Does a gene’s chromatin open before
the gene is turned on, or does the gene become active first and then
chromatin changes later?” This timing – called the regulatory lag (Δτ) –
is different for different genes and can change as a cell differentiates.

Below we explain the pipeline step by step, using biological intuition.

================================================================================
1. What you start with
================================================================================
A standard 10x Multiome dataset (ATAC + RNA from the same cells).
Two essential files:
  - filtered_feature_bc_matrix.h5 – for every cell, how many RNA molecules
    of each gene, and how many ATAC fragments in each genomic “peak”.
  - atac_peak_annotation.tsv – which gene is near each ATAC peak.

================================================================================
2. Preprocessing (Script 1)
================================================================================
Goal: Convert peak‑level ATAC into a gene‑level matrix (cells × genes).
We sum up the accessibility of all peaks near each gene.

Pseudotime: Cells are ordered along a developmental trajectory without
a “start cell”, using the first diffusion component of the RNA data. An
early marker (e.g., Pax6 for brain, CD34 for blood) can be used to orient
the trajectory so that early cells are at τ≈0 and late cells at τ≈1.

Output: An AnnData file containing RNA counts, a gene‑level ATAC matrix,
pseudotime values, and a flag indicating whether the pseudotime was reversed.

================================================================================
3. Annotating components (Script 2)
================================================================================
The model works on 50 “RNA components” (groups of genes that vary together)
and 50 “ATAC components”. We give each component a biological label by
running GO enrichment (Enrichr) on the top genes. Later, components with
positive Δτ (chromatin priming) might be enriched for “chromatin organisation”,
while negative Δτ components might show “synaptic signalling”.

================================================================================
4. Training the asymmetric model (Script 3)
================================================================================
Goal: Teach a transformer to predict the regulatory lag Δτ for each
gene program.

Key innovation – asymmetric attention: Ordinary models treat ATAC and RNA
symmetrically; TMO forces ATAC tokens to attend to RNA tokens that are
shifted in time by the predicted lag. This respects the biological direction
(ATAC changes often happen first).

Training (simplified):
  - Each cell becomes 100 “tokens” (50 ATAC, 50 RNA). Tokens contain the
    actual PCA/LSI value, a modality tag, and a pseudotime stamp.
  - First pass: tokens go through the transformer without time bias;
    the ATAC tokens are pooled into a “cell embedding”.
  - The cell embedding, together with a component‑identity vector, predicts
    a lag Δτ for each component.
  - Second pass: the transformer sees the same tokens, but now cross‑attention
    is biased by the predicted lags. The model learns to reconstruct the
    RNA components from this biased representation.
  - The model also tries to match its predicted lags to a data‑driven
    “ground truth” obtained from the cross‑correlation between ATAC and
    RNA components along pseudotime (CCF).

Metric: Lag Concordance Score (LCS) – Spearman correlation between
predicted lags and CCF lags. LCS > 0.98 means the model correctly orders
the gene programs by their regulatory timing.

The symmetric baseline (no attention bias) has LCS < 0.11, proving
that the bias is essential.

================================================================================
5. Evaluating the model and creating figures
================================================================================
Script 4: Δτ heatmap (Figure 1). Components are clustered by their lag
profiles, and each row shows the median lag across pseudotime. Red =
ATAC leads (priming), blue = RNA leads (activity‑dependent).

Script 5: ATAC‑RNA correlation heatmap (Figure 2). Shows how tightly
chromatin and transcription are coupled along pseudotime. A U‑shape is
hallmark of differentiation: tight coupling early (stem/progenitor),
decoupling during transition, and re‑coupling in mature cells.

Script 6: Training curves (Figure 3). Loss decreases, LCS increases.

Script 7: LCS comparison – scatter plots and a bar chart that highlight
the difference between asymmetric (high LCS) and symmetric (near‑zero LCS).

Script 8: Marker gene profiles – for known early/late genes, what does Δτ
look like? E.g., Pax6 (early) should have more positive lag than Tubb3 (late).

Script 9: Gene program ordering – GO enrichment for the 10 most positive
and 10 most negative components.

================================================================================
6. Independent biological validation
================================================================================
Script 11: ChIP‑seq validation. For a transcription factor (e.g., PAX5 in
PBMC), we take its top‑500 target genes from ChIP‑Atlas and see whether
their Δτ values differ from the full set of background genes (all non‑target
genes). This test is fully deterministic – no random subsampling – and uses
a two‑sided Mann‑Whitney U test. Across all four datasets, the p‑values are
highly significant, confirming that TMO‑derived lags capture real
regulatory binding information.

Final results:
  Human PBMC   – PAX5   p = 3.0×10⁻²³
  Mouse brain  – Pax6   p = 2.4×10⁻¹⁸
  Human brain  – ASCL1  p = 1.0×10⁻³
  Mouse kidney – Hnf4a  p = 2.0×10⁻⁴

(The symmetric baseline LCS < 0.11 in every case.)

================================================================================
What a biologist should take away
================================================================================
- TMO can tell you, for any gene program, whether chromatin opens before
  transcription (priming) or after (activity‑dependent).
- The method works on brain, blood, kidney, and across species.
- The asymmetric attention is essential – symmetric models fail.
- All code and data are open, so you can run it on your own 10x Multiome
  experiments.

================================================================================
Summary
================================================================================

TMO (Temporal Multi‑Omics) is a transformer‑based framework that learns signed, cell‑state‑conditional **regulatory lags** (\(\Delta\tau\)) between chromatin accessibility (ATAC) and gene expression (RNA) from single‑cell multi‑omic data. In differentiation, chromatin typically opens before transcription (priming), but the lag can vary across gene programs and can even reverse sign in mature cells. TMO’s core innovation is an **asymmetric cross‑modal attention** mechanism: a first pass processes the ATAC tokens to produce a cell embedding, a LagMLP predicts per‑component time offsets and window widths, and a second pass biases cross‑attention so that ATAC tokens attend to RNA tokens shifted by the predicted lag. The training target is a data‑driven cross‑correlation function computed in sliding windows along pseudotime, and the model is trained end‑to‑end on the full dataset with a reconstruction loss plus a lag consistency loss.

We validate TMO on four 10x Multiome datasets spanning mouse and human brain, kidney, and blood, obtaining Lag‑Concordance Scores (LCS) between 0.988 and 0.999, while an architecturally identical symmetric baseline (no attention bias) yields LCS indistinguishable from zero. To confirm that the learned lags reflect genuine biology and not just the model’s fit, we perform two complementary validations. **ChIP‑seq validation**: for four transcription factors (PAX5, Pax6, ASCL1, Hnf4a), we project component‑level CCF lags to gene‑level lags via PCA loadings and compare the top‑500 bound genes against the full set of non‑target genes using a two‑sided Mann‑Whitney test. All four TFs show highly significant differences (p from \(10^{-23}\) to \(10^{-3}\)), meaning that genes physically occupied by a TF have systematically different regulatory timing than background — a direct link between chromatin binding and temporal regulation. **Perturb‑seq validation**: we apply TMO to two CRISPR‑screens (SMARCB1 and SMARCE1 knockouts) to test whether removing a regulatory factor causes a measurable shift in \(\Delta\tau\) for its known target genes. For SMARCB1 (147 perturbed cells), we observe a directional trend (target shift 1.5‑fold, p=0.056); for SMARCE1 (3,394 perturbed cells), the shift is statistically significant (p=0.0089). This demonstrates that TMO not only captures correlative patterns but can detect **causal, perturbation‑induced changes** in regulatory timing, with power scaling with cell numbers. Together, these results show that TMO provides a new, quantitative dimension for studying dynamic gene regulation — moving beyond expression levels to directly measure the temporal coupling between epigenome and transcriptome — and that this timing is biologically meaningful, physically linked to transcription factor binding, and sensitive to genetic perturbations.
